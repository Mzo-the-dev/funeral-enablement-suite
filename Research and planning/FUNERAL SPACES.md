13. Executive summary
    Funeral Spaces is a proposed digital platform designed to improve the experience of planning and administering a funeral in South Africa. The concept starts with the administrative needs of self-managed funeral societies and expands to connect funeral parlours, families and relevant suppliers.
    The key idea is not to create several disconnected applications. It is to provide secure, role-specific workspaces—called Spaces—that can connect through a shared funeral case when the relevant parties need to collaborate.

Why this could matter
Societies need reliable records for contributions, covered lives, committee decisions and funeral benefits.
Parlours need better ways to present services, quote customers, manage bookings, invoice clients and track payments.
Families need clear guidance and coordination during a stressful period, without repeating the same information to multiple organisations.
Suppliers need a structured way to receive relevant orders and coordinate delivery.
The platform could create value by connecting these needs, rather than offering only generic member administration. 2. The Funeral Spaces concept
A Space is a secure digital workspace belonging to an organisation or individual. Each Space has its own records, permissions, workflows and services. Spaces can connect when a funeral case requires cooperation, with access limited to information needed for that task.

3. The four core Spaces
   3.1 Society Space
   The Society Space is the initial foundation. It should help a funeral society operate with greater accuracy and transparency without forcing it to change its banking arrangements.
   Society registration, constitution and configurable rules.
   Member registration and covered-lives/family register, including relationship, effective dates and authorised changes.
   Monthly contribution schedules, arrears tracking, receipts and transaction histories.
   Bank statement import and reconciliation, with uncertain matches flagged for review.
   Committee roles, permissions and withdrawal approval records.
   Funeral notification, eligibility checks against configured rules, document checklist and claim tracking.
   Monthly statements, financial reports, audit history and member-facing contribution visibility.
   Notifications for payments, arrears, membership changes, approvals and funeral cases.
   3.2 Parlour Space
   Verified business profile, service area, contact details and operating information.
   Funeral packages and optional services with transparent itemised pricing.
   Quotation requests, quote versions, customer approvals and booking confirmation.
   Invoices, payment links and payment reconciliation against the correct invoice.
   Case management, outstanding documents, tasks and service milestones.
   Order coordination with participating suppliers.
   Basic operational reporting and customer communication history.
   3.3 Family Space
   Private funeral case with a guided checklist and clear next steps.
   Ability to invite selected relatives and assign tasks.
   Document wallet for relevant membership, policy, identity and claim documents.
   Claim progress, outstanding requirements and approved status updates.
   Parlour discovery, comparable quotations, selected services and booking details.
   Payment links, invoice status and receipts.
   A record of important decisions, dates and arrangements.
   3.4 Supplier Space
   Supplier profile and services offered, including service area and availability.
   Catalogue for relevant items such as flowers, catering, tents, chairs, transport, venues and tombstones.
   Quote or order acceptance, delivery/fulfilment status and communication.
   Invoices and payment status, subject to the selected payment arrangement.
   Limited access to only the case details needed to fulfil the order.
4. The connected funeral journey
   The platform’s differentiator should be the connections between Spaces. The user should not have to restart the process every time a different organisation becomes involved.

5. Intelligent features and differentiation
   5.1 Guided funeral journey
   A guided workflow should ask a small number of essential questions, then generate a tailored checklist based on the circumstances. A society benefit, formal insurance claim and self-funded funeral can have different requirements. The product should distinguish them.
   5.2 Secure funeral document wallet
   Store and organise relevant documents with purpose-specific sharing, access logs, clear permissions and retention controls. The system can identify missing documents for a specific workflow rather than merely storing files.
   5.3 Transparent marketplace
   Allow participating parlours to list packages and services, and help families compare itemised quotations. Show inclusions, exclusions, optional extras, service areas and payment terms so users can compare like-for-like offers.
   5.4 Payment coordination
   Link payments to a clearly identified recipient and a specific contribution, invoice or order. Show the difference between payment initiated, pending, confirmed, failed and refunded. Prevent duplicate posting and reconcile payments to the correct account. Initially use approved payment providers and avoid taking custody of funds.
   5.5 Family coordination
   Let a family invite selected relatives, assign tasks, share approved documents, track dates and record decisions. Keep family coordination separate from the society’s confidential committee and financial records.
   5.6 Funeral assistant
   A future assistant could answer questions in plain language, explain the next steps, retrieve authorised society rules, identify missing documents and help initiate workflows. It should state uncertainty clearly, cite the relevant rules where possible and route decisions to authorised people.

Do not make artificial intelligence the centre of the first release. The most valuable early intelligence will come from accurate records, configurable rules and dependable automation. AI can be introduced later as a guided interface on top of those foundations.

This section is a product-planning overview, not legal advice. Obtain South African legal and regulatory guidance before launch, especially before enabling payment initiation, handling funds, arranging insurance or making benefit decisions.
POPIA and sensitive records
Collect only the information needed for a defined purpose.
Define lawful processing, access, correction, retention and deletion procedures.
Use strong authentication, encryption, role-based permissions and access logs.
Provide clear consent and sharing controls where applicable.
Create incident response and data-breach procedures.
Financial services and funds
Keep society funds in the society’s existing bank account during the initial phase.
Distinguish internal approval records from bank-authorised transfers.
Use an appropriately authorised payment provider for payment processing.
Obtain advice before holding funds, pooling contributions, initiating transfers or introducing insurance products.
Do not assume that every society’s benefit arrangement has the same legal status.
Rules and claims
Let each society approve and configure its own constitution and benefit rules.
Show the basis for eligibility checks and preserve relevant records.
Route exceptions and disputes to authorised people.
Do not promise approval or present a software flag as a binding insurance decision. 11. Validation plan and pilot metrics
The next step should be customer discovery and prototype testing, not a large development commitment. Interview the people who perform the work and observe their current process.

These are proposed experiment targets, not industry benchmarks. Funeral claims are irregular, so a short pilot cannot prove long-term claims performance or retention. Continue tracking use and trust over several months. 12. Strategic recommendation

The strongest initial proposition
“Every member’s contributions are traceable, every covered life is recorded, and every funeral claim has a documented outcome—then the family can connect to a participating parlour through one guided journey.”
Immediate next steps
Choose a narrow first customer segment: self-managed societies with recurring contributions and meaningful administration needs.
Interview 15–20 societies and document their actual processes.
Test the highest-value workflows: contribution reconciliation, covered lives and funeral claim tracking.
Recruit 3–5 pilot societies before committing to a full build.
Speak to a small number of funeral parlours about quotation, booking and payment pain points.
Get legal advice on privacy, society benefit arrangements and the boundaries of payment/insurance functionality.
Measure adoption and willingness to pay before expanding to a marketplace.
